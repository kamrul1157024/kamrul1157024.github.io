Lessons
Slide 1

Local rule №1: multiply

Zoom into one gate: s = w·x. A gradient g arrives at s from the right ("L changes this much when s changes"). The gate hands it backward to both inputs — but each one scaled by the other input. Step through it.

MULTIPLY RULE
w's gradient is the upstream gradient scaled by the other input — nudge w by ε and s moves by x·ε.
Slide 2

Local rule №2: addition is distribution

The gate y = s + b does nothing to the gradient at all — it photocopies it. One gradient g arrives at y; two identical copies leave, one down each input branch. Watch it split.

ADD RULE — GRADIENT DISTRIBUTES
and the mirror image: when one value feeds N different places, its total gradient is the sum of all N copies coming back. Distribution forward, summation backward. (Slide 7 uses this.)
Slide 3

Two more rules: subtract and square

The last two gates of the graph. Subtract (e = y − t) is add's sibling: the gradient copies to the first input and copies negated to the second. Square (L = e²) scales the incoming gradient by 2e — the only other gate that changes it. Step through square first (it feeds the subtract gate), then subtract.

L = e²
e = y − t
SUBTRACT & SQUARE
four gates, four rules: multiply scales by the other input, add copies, subtract copies with a sign, square scales by 2·input. That is the entire vocabulary of backprop.
Slide 4

The chain: every gate does its one rule

All four rules, one graph: y = w·x + b, loss L = (y − t)². Move the sliders and step it forward — then step it backward: the square gate scales the gradient, the subtract gate signs it, the add gate copies it, the multiply gate scales it again. What arrives at w is the chain rule — one multiplication per gate on the path.

CHAIN RULE — the gradient at w is the product of the local rules along the path:
nothing else exists. Every backprop in every framework is these local rules, composed.
Slide 5

The formula morphs: one weight → a vector of weights

A neuron has four inputs and four weights: y = x₀w₀ + x₁w₁ + x₂w₂ + x₃w₃ + b. Press Morph ▸ and watch the multiply rule from slide 1 change shape — spoiler: it doesn't. Only the subscripts do.

DOT PRODUCT RULE
wᵢ appears in exactly one product, so its gradient touches exactly one input: xᵢ. The add gates distribute g to every product; each multiply gate scales it by its own xᵢ.
Slide 6

The formula morphs again: vectors → matrices

Now B=8 samples and M=3 outputs at once: Y = X·W + b. The gradient formula must morph too — press Morph ▸. The interesting moment: the product only fits together one way, and that's where the transpose comes from. It's not a new rule — it's the multiply rule with shapes that line up.

THE MATRIX RULES
gradient w.r.t. a factor = upstream gradient × the OTHER factor, transposed so inner dimensions cancel — slide 1's multiply rule, summed over the batch.
Slide 7

Distribution in reverse — and LoRA

The bias b is added to all 8 rows of Y. Slide 2 said a value used in N places collects N gradient copies that sum — so ∂L/∂b = Σ_b G. Step through the copies converging into one. Then the same chain with a constraint: LoRA freezes W and learns a rank-r update A·B instead.

WHY LORA WORKS
The price: if the needed update isn't low-rank, LoRA plateaus where full fine-tuning would keep going. The payoff: a fraction of the parameters, one frozen base model, many swappable adapters.
Backpropagation is four local rules — multiply, add, subtract, square — chained over a computation graph. Addition distributes the gradient; multiplication scales it by the other input; matrices only add shape bookkeeping. LoRA is the same chain with a rank constraint on where it may act.
